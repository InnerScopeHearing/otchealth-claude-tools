# Automated QA for Correct Audio Playback in iOS Apps (with a Hearing-Screening Lens)

## Executive Summary

**The honest answer: there is no mature, widely-adopted, drop-in CI tool that verifies "the actual sound coming out of an iPhone's speaker/headphones is the right frequency at the right level."** This is a genuinely hard, under-tooled corner of mobile QA, and iHEARtest is not missing some well-known solution — it largely doesn't exist as a packaged product. What does exist breaks into four real tiers of decreasing reach and increasing manual effort:

1. **In-process buffer verification (the strongest automatable layer, and the one iHEARtest should build first).** `AVAudioEngine`'s `installTap(onBus:bufferSize:format:block:)` lets you capture the actual PCM samples the app hands to the audio graph *before* they reach hardware, run FFT (Accelerate/vDSP) on them, and assert frequency/amplitude in an XCTest — no simulator audio routing, no external hardware. This proves "the app generated a 1000 Hz tone at the requested dB gain," which is the correctness half of the problem, but it can never prove what the *speaker or Bluetooth device actually reproduced* (DAC response, amp gain staging, AirPods codec, hardware volume curve).
2. **Route/session-state testing (fully automatable, commonly done via a thin wrapper).** `AVAudioSession` itself isn't natively mockable — Apple gives no first-party test seam — but the standard pattern is to wrap `AVAudioSession` behind a protocol/injectable interface so your routing *logic* (AirPods-detected → do X, receiver vs. speaker → do Y) is unit-testable, while the real `AVAudioSession.currentRoute`/route-change-notification behavior is only trustworthy on a real device, since Simulator explicitly does not model inter-app audio session interaction or most route changes.
3. **Captured-output analysis (technically possible on macOS/Simulator, brittle and rarely used in real CI).** Tools like the Appium XCUITest driver's audio-capture feature exist and work by routing Simulator or real-device audio out through a virtual macOS audio device (historically Soundflower, now typically BlackHole) and recording it with ffmpeg; `xcrun simctl io booted recordVideo` also captures Simulator audio+video to a file (audio capture in this command depends on the Xcode version). Once you have a WAV/video file you can FFT it (scipy, aubio) or fingerprint-compare it to a golden reference (the Appium Pro two-part series does exactly this). This is real and automatable, but it captures the Simulator's synthesized/virtual audio path or a wired real-device tap, not what physically radiates from a speaker into air — so it validates the software pipeline, not the acoustic result, and none of it is a supported, CTO-endorsed pattern for real-device Depot CI.
4. **Physical acoustic verification (the only way to validate the real-world output, and it is NOT automated anywhere at consumer/prosumer scale).** Clinical audiometer manufacturers (SHOEBOX, hearX/hearScreen, hearX hearTest) do not solve this in software either — they ship standardized, pre-calibrated hardware/headphone combinations and require **periodic physical recalibration with a reference sound-level meter and artificial-ear coupler** (annual is typical; some occupational contexts require quarterly), governed by IEC 60645 / ANSI S3.6. Consumer-grade "hearing test" apps that run on arbitrary phones and headphones use **biological calibration** instead — an assumed-normal-hearing tester sets the reference 0 dB HL point by ear (Bekesy tracking method), which the literature reports as accurate to roughly ±1 dB at the calibration points but with 7–11 dB standard deviation versus a clinical audiometer overall. Room/loudspeaker calibration tools like REW support SPL-meter calibration workflows, but they are for acoustic measurement rigs, not mobile CI, and have no documented CLI/automation story.

**Bottom line for iHEARtest:** treat this as two separate, honestly-scoped problems. (A) "Did the app correctly compute and hand off the intended waveform/gain to the OS" — this is fully unit-testable today via `AVAudioEngine` taps + FFT, and iHEARtest should build this if it hasn't (a `Tiers`-style module for audio generation, with a spec asserting the tapped buffer's dominant frequency and RMS amplitude match the requested test tone within tolerance). (B) "Did the physical sound reaching the ear match the calibrated level" — this is not something CI can verify for an arbitrary consumer iPhone + arbitrary headphones, full stop; the entire clinical-audiometry industry's answer is standardized/certified hardware plus periodic human-in-the-loop SPL-meter calibration, and any consumer hearing-screening app (including iHEARtest's own category as a PSAP/screening tool, not a certified audiometer) inherits that same limitation. The honest QA posture is: automate everything in tier 1–2 in CI, do NOT invest engineering time chasing tier-3 tricks (Soundflower/BlackHole/ffmpeg loopback) for iHEARtest's real device pipeline since it's brittle, macOS-only tooling not built for Depot-style headless CI and doesn't validate the acoustic result anyway, and document tier 4 (device-model output-level variance) as a known, industry-wide, unsolved-by-software limitation — mitigated only by user-facing calibration/headphone-guidance UX, not by a CI gate.

## Detailed Findings

### 1. Can you programmatically capture and FFT-analyze an iOS app's actual audio OUTPUT in CI?

**Simulator side:**
- `xcrun simctl io booted recordVideo <file>` records Simulator video, and **audio capture is bundled in by default starting Xcode 15+**; on older Xcode it was video-only. [Sarunw](https://sarunw.com/posts/take-screenshot-and-record-video-in-ios-simulator/), [Screenify Studio](https://www.screenify.studio/blog/2026-04-19-record-xcode-simulator), [Apple dev forum thread on early recordVideo audio gaps](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/109407)
- Once you have that captured file you can run any FFT/pitch-detection tool against it — `scipy.fft`/`scipy.signal.find_peaks` in Python ([Renée Desporte's writeup](https://www.reneedesporte.com/2024/08/02/detecting-frequencies-in-audio-signals-with-python/), [endolith's frequency-estimation gist](https://gist.github.com/endolith/255291)), or the CLI tool `aubio` (`aubiopitch -u Hz`) for straight pitch/frequency extraction ([aubio manual](https://aubio.org/manual/latest/cli.html)). Neither is iOS-specific; they're generic signal-processing tools you'd bolt onto a captured WAV.
- The Appium XCUITest driver ships a first-class `mobile: startAudioRecording` / `stopAudioRecording` feature specifically for this: on **Simulator** it routes audio through a virtual macOS audio device (documented historically as Soundflower; BlackHole is the modern maintained equivalent — [BlackHole GitHub](https://github.com/ExistentialAudio/BlackHole)) and records with ffmpeg; on **real device** it requires a physical cable into a Mac via Audio MIDI Setup. Apple explicitly disallows redirecting phone-call audio this way — only app/system sound. [Appium XCUITest audio-capture docs](https://appium.github.io/appium-xcuitest-driver/11.0/guides/audio-capture/)
- The **Appium Pro two-part series** is the closest thing to an end-to-end "how do I actually do this" tutorial: Part 1 sets up ffmpeg + Soundflower/emulator audio routing and captures a WAV/MP4 during a scripted test run; Part 2 verifies the capture by **audio-fingerprint-comparing it against a saved "golden" reference clip** rather than doing raw FFT assertions, because exact-sample comparison is too brittle across runs. [Part 1](https://appiumpro.com/editions/69-capturing-audio-output-during-testing-part-1), [Part 2](https://www.headspin.io/blog/capturing-audio-output-during-testing-part-2)

**Real device side:**
- Everything above that targets real devices needs a **physical cable/audio interface loopback** into the host Mac — there is no wireless/headless equivalent, and nothing here is something you'd run unattended in a Depot macOS GitHub Actions runner (no operator present to cable a phone). AWS Device Farm, the most common cloud real-device farm, documents video capture and activity logs for remote-access sessions but **does not document a separate audio-capture feature** for physical-device runs. [AWS Device Farm remote access docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/devicefarm/latest/developerguide/remote-access.html)

**Net assessment:** technically capturable on Simulator (and cabled real device) with 2019-era Appium tooling that is no longer actively a "modern CI-native" pattern — it's macOS-desktop-audio plumbing (virtual device drivers, ffmpeg) that predates and is orthogonal to headless cloud macOS runners like Depot. Nobody in the search results describes this running unattended in a modern CI/CD pipeline; it's demonstrated as a scripted local/lab workflow.

### 2. In-process buffer capture: the strongest lever, and it needs no Simulator-audio tricks at all

The single most useful, actually-CI-friendly technique found: **`AVAudioEngine.installTap(onBus:bufferSize:format:block:)`**. This installs a callback that receives the real `AVAudioPCMBuffer` flowing through a node (e.g., the main mixer, or directly on your tone-generator node) — the *exact samples the app is about to hand to CoreAudio* — with no virtual device, no ffmpeg, no macOS audio routing at all. [Apple dev forum thread on installTap for testing](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/649693)

- Recommended test pattern from the forums: don't drive it off the microphone in tests — **inject an `AVAudioPlayerNode` playing a buffer you constructed** (e.g., a synthetic 1000 Hz sine at a known dB) as the "input," tap its output, and assert on the tapped buffer. This is a pure, deterministic, hardware-independent unit test.
- Caveats to design around: the tap buffer format must exactly match the node's output/connection format, or you silently get zeroed/garbage buffers ([forum thread on this exact footgun](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/689452)); requested buffer sizes below ~4800 frames get silently rounded up by the engine ([forum thread](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/797033)).
- For the actual frequency/amplitude math once you have the buffer: Apple's **Accelerate/vDSP** framework (`vDSP_fft_zrip`) is the standard on-device FFT primitive, described in detail in a walkthrough on visualizing iOS audio spectra via Accelerate ([myuiviews.com](https://www.myuiviews.com/2016/03/04/visualizing-audio-frequency-spectrum-on-ios-via-accelerate-vdsp-fast-fourier-transform.html)), and there's an open-source real-time FFT helper library built on the same primitives ([tomer8007/real-time-audio-fft](https://github.com/tomer8007/real-time-audio-fft)) that could be vendored straight into a test target rather than reimplemented.
- This is the layer where a `qa/unit/` spec like `"generating a 1000 Hz / -20 dBFS test tone produces a tapped buffer whose dominant FFT bin is within ±2% of 1000 Hz and whose RMS matches the target gain within ±0.5 dB"` is fully achievable, deterministic, fast, and runs on any CI runner (no macOS-audio-hardware dependency at all, since the tap intercepts before hardware).
- **What this deliberately does NOT prove:** that the phone's DAC, amplifier, speaker driver, or a connected AirPods pair reproduces that buffer faithfully. It proves app-layer correctness, not acoustic correctness.

### 3. AVAudioSession routing logic: testable via dependency injection, NOT via Apple-provided mocks

- Apple provides **no official unit-testing seam** for `AVAudioSession` — no mock class, no protocol Apple ships for injection. The standard approach identified across the forums and engineering write-ups is to build your **own thin protocol wrapper** around the small slice of `AVAudioSession` your app actually calls (category/mode setting, `currentRoute`, route-change-notification handling) and inject a test double that returns canned `AVAudioSessionPortDescription`/route values, so your *decision logic* ("if `outputs` contains `.bluetoothA2DP` do X") is unit-tested without touching the real session. [Responding to audio route changes — Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudiosession/responding_to_audio_session_route_changes), [Simform engineering write-up on route/device switching](https://medium.com/simform-engineering/audio-input-device-switch-management-in-avaudiosession-4a7c4dd78eb5)
- Real route detection (did AirPods actually get picked up, did the silent switch actually mute) is explicitly **not reliable in Simulator** — Apple's own docs and multiple forum threads state Simulator does not model inter-process audio session interaction or most route changes, and recommend testing route behavior only on real hardware ([BrowserStack Appium guide](https://www.browserstack.com/guide/appium-ios-simulator-vs-real-device-testing), [Apple "Configuring Device Hardware" guide](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/documentation/Audio/Conceptual/AudioSessionProgrammingGuide/OptimizingForDeviceHardware/OptimizingForDeviceHardware.html)). There's even a documented case of Simulator silently *skipping* real-hardware-only audio processing paths entirely (AVSpeechSynthesizer's on-device hearing-aid-style EQ/noise-reduction graph only activates on real hardware, never in Simulator — [Medium write-up](https://medium.com/@info_4533/why-avspeechsynthesizer-sounds-terrible-on-real-iphones-eb4565862ea8)), which is a direct, cautionary analog to iHEARtest's own AVAudioSession `.measurement` / echoCancellation-disabled requirements documented in the Companion app's `CLAUDE.md` gotchas — this class of correctness bug is fundamentally simulator-invisible and can only be caught by device-only manual/TestFlight QA, exactly as iHEARtest's own CLAUDE.md already states ("Device-only classes of bugs... can only be verified there").

### 4. How real audiometry/hearing-test software handles this — the clinical and consumer-app literature

This is the most directly load-bearing finding for iHEARtest, because it shows the entire industry's answer is **not** "better automated QA software" — it's standardized hardware plus scheduled human recalibration:

- **Certified clinical audiometers** (SHOEBOX, hearX hearScreen/hearTest) are validated against **IEC 60645** (audiometer test-signal/output standards) and **ANSI S3.6**, and require **periodic physical recalibration using a reference sound-level meter and an artificial-ear/coupler** — SHOEBOX bundles "annual calibration" into its customer service package, and hearX documents calibration intervals that can be quarterly for some occupational-health deployments. [SHOEBOX PureTest](https://www.shoebox.md/products/shoebox-puretest/), [hearX hearScreen](https://hearxgroup.com/products/hearscreen), [hearX Portable Audiometric Screening review PDF](https://www.hearxgroup.com/assets/documents/PortableAudiometricScreening.pdf)
- Crucially, these products get around the "arbitrary consumer hardware" problem by **not using arbitrary hardware** — they ship a specific, pre-characterized phone/tablet + specific pre-characterized calibrated headphones as a bundle, and the calibration is a property of that exact hardware pairing, not of "software running on any iPhone."
- **Consumer-grade smartphone hearing-test apps** (the ones closer to iHEARtest's actual deployment model — arbitrary iPhone, arbitrary headphones/AirPods) solve the "we can't physically calibrate every user's device" problem with **biological calibration**: a person with presumed-normal hearing (age 18–35, no hearing-loss history) sets the 0 dB HL reference point using a Bekesy-tracking self-test, and the app uses that as its output-level reference going forward. Peer-reviewed comparisons found this method accurate to within ~1 dB of reference-equivalent threshold levels at the calibration points, but with **7.3–10.7 dB standard deviation versus true clinical audiometry** across the full protocol — a materially wider error band than clinical-grade hardware calibration. [PMC: Hearing Tests on Mobile Devices — biological calibration](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4906240/), [PMC: Biologically Calibrated Mobile Devices vs Pure-Tone Audiometry](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5784183/), [PMC: Android free-app hearing-loss assessment reliability](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11306450/), [Dove Press: Hearing Test App for Android — pure-tone audiometry distinctive features](https://www.dovepress.com/the-hearing-test-app-for-android-devices-distinctive-features-of-pure--peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-MDER)
- None of this literature describes an **automated software QA/CI process** for verifying calibration correctness on an ongoing basis — every source describes calibration as either (a) a one-time or periodic *manufacturing/service* event done with physical instruments, or (b) a *per-user self-calibration step done at test time* (biological calibration), not a CI-pipeline concern at all. This is strong evidence that "automated CI verification of acoustic output correctness" is not a solved problem anywhere in this space, clinical or consumer.
- General-purpose acoustic measurement software (**Room EQ Wizard / REW**) does support SPL-meter-based calibration workflows (playing a reference tone, entering the meter's dB reading, or loading a mic calibration file), but this is manual, human-in-the-loop lab tooling for room/speaker acoustics — there is no documented CLI/scriptable/CI-automatable interface. [REW SPL calibration docs](https://www.roomeqwizard.com/help/help_en-GB/html/inputcal.html)

### 5. Adjacent professional-audio validation tooling (context, not a direct fit)

- Apple's own **`auval`/`auvaltool`** (Audio Unit Validation) is the closest thing to an official Apple CLI audio-correctness tool, but it validates that an **AudioUnit plugin correctly implements the AU API contract** (instantiation, channel config negotiation, real-time-safety, race conditions) — it explicitly does **not** assess audio quality or implementation correctness of the DSP itself, and it's a macOS/plugin-host concept, not applicable to a Capacitor/vanilla-JS iOS app like iHEARtest. [Apple TN2204](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/technotes/tn2204/_index.html), [Moonbase auval walkthrough](https://moonbase.sh/articles/debugging-your-audio-unit-plugin-with-auval-aka-auvaltool/)
- Searches for how large consumer audio-first companies (Spotify, Shazam, Dolby) handle this in public engineering blogs came up essentially empty of anything iOS-audio-output-specific — the closest hits were generic "we run automated tests in CI" QA-culture posts and Shazam's *audio-fingerprinting* algorithm itself (which is a recognition technique, not a QA-verification technique). This reinforces the conclusion: there is no well-known industry playbook for this being casually reused; if it existed as common practice at consumer-audio-scale companies, it would show up in their engineering blogs, and it doesn't.

## Recommendation for iHEARtest specifically

Given iHEARtest's actual architecture (vanilla JS/Capacitor, Web Audio-based tone generation per the app's own compliance/PHI rules, tested via Vitest in `qa/unit/`), the actionable, CI-native slice of all of the above is:

1. **Build a deterministic tone-generation correctness spec** at the JS/Web-Audio layer (analogous to the native `AVAudioEngine` tap pattern, but for `OscillatorNode`/`AudioBufferSourceNode`): generate the tone off-line into an `AudioBuffer`/`Float32Array`, run an FFT (a small JS FFT lib, or Node's `fft-js`/similar, mirroring the vDSP approach) against the raw samples in a Vitest spec, and assert the dominant frequency and gain match the requested test-tone parameters within tolerance. This is the direct equivalent of tier 1 above and is fully achievable today with no native/macOS dependency.
2. **Do not invest in Soundflower/BlackHole/ffmpeg-loopback Simulator capture** for iHEARtest's CI — it's unmaintained-feeling 2019-era tooling built around desktop audio routing, doesn't run cleanly headless on Depot macOS runners, and even if wired up would only validate the Simulator's software audio path, not the acoustic correctness that actually matters for a hearing-screening tool.
3. **Keep leaning on the Mark-review + real-device TestFlight ritual for anything acoustic** (AVAudioSession category/mode correctness, actual output level per device model, AirPods/Bluetooth routing, silent-switch behavior) — this is already iHEARtest's practice and it is, per the research above, genuinely the correct practice, not a stopgap; this is what the clinical audiometer industry itself falls back on (device-specific calibration + human verification), just formalized as periodic hardware recalibration with an SPL meter rather than a single hearing professional's device-by-device review.
4. **Consider documenting, in the compliance/QA docs, an explicit statement that per-device-model output-level variance is a known, industry-wide, categorically-unresolved-by-software limitation** (not an iHEARtest engineering gap) — this protects against a future reviewer or auditor assuming "why isn't this automated" without understanding that no comparable consumer hearing app has solved it either.
